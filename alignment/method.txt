Cross-dataset cell type assignment

Cell type identification in connectomes has largely relied on morphological similarity, typically via NBLAST (Costa et al., 2016). While effective for morphologically distinctive neurons, this approach fails where multiple types share similar arbor geometry but differ in their synaptic partners — a pervasive situation among columnar visual neurons and many central brain interneurons. Connectivity-based methods such as NTAC (Schwartzman et al., 2025) address this by propagating labels within a single connectome's graph, but struggle when the graph is only partially labelled, as the discriminative type-level connectivity patterns needed to distinguish similar types have not yet been resolved. In practice, human annotators resolve such ambiguities by considering morphology first, then refining assignments using connectivity context. We formalised this two-stage reasoning as an iterative cross-dataset alignment algorithm that transfers cell type labels from the fully annotated FAFB/FlyWire connectome (Dorkenwald et al., 2024; Schlegel et al., 2024) to BANC. NBLAST morphological scores provide initial type hypotheses and constrain the search space; at each iteration, BANC neurons' connectivity profiles are compared to fixed FAFB type-level reference centroids (mean input/output connectivity at 1-hop and 2-hop levels, scored via a cosine–weighted Jaccard ensemble metric), and the resulting connectivity scores are blended with NBLAST scores using an adaptive weight (alpha) that ramps from 0.05 (NBLAST-dominant) to 0.95 (connectivity-dominant) over 80 iterations (Zhu & Ghahramani, 2002). Hard assignments are made via a capacity-constrained greedy procedure: neurons with NBLAST data are first matched to individual FAFB neurons (each claimable by at most one BANC neuron), then remaining neurons are assigned to types by soft probability ranking, with per-type capacity limits derived from FAFB left-right count variability. A curated list of 1,125 known-incorrect NBLAST matches was excluded from the assignment pool. A soma-presence rule prevented neurons with a detected nucleus from being assigned to types whose somata lie outside the CNS volume (e.g., photoreceptors, lamina neurons). The best iteration by holdout accuracy was retained via early stopping.

We applied this algorithm to 133,120 proofread BANC neurons across 8,816 FAFB cell types (whole brain, bilateral). Of these, 81,110 (60.9%) had existing cell type annotations and served as Tier-1 anchors; 41,911 (31.5%) lacked annotations but had NBLAST matches providing soft initialisation (Tier 2); and 10,100 (7.6%) had neither and began unassigned (Tier 3). Coverage was asymmetric across hemispheres: on the right, 3,139 of 61,175 neurons (5.1%) lacked cell type annotations, while on the left, 11,660 of 58,119 (20.1%) were untyped, reflecting the historical concentration of manual annotation effort on the right side. In the optic lobes specifically, 11,655 of 93,307 neurons (12.5%) were untyped — 3,113/41,934 (7.4%) on the right and 11,732/39,086 (30.0%) on the left — across 685 FAFB optic lobe types. On the right optic lobe, the algorithm achieved 82.7% holdout accuracy (vs. 43.9% for NBLAST alone and 65.0% for NTAC), with 90.4% accuracy on the benchmark type Mi1 and 0.938 Spearman correlation between BANC and FAFB per-type neuron count distributions.
